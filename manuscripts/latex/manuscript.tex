\papertitle{Population reconstruction in acute human wounds: held-out time points, donor transfer and baseline sensitivity}
\section*{Abstract}
\noindent A smooth trajectory fitted to cross-sectional single-cell data does not establish prediction of an individual's wound course. We tested whether population fields improve on no motion when a target time point or donor is excluded from fitting.
Three healthy donors contributed intact skin and acute wounds at days 1, 7 and 30. A frozen expression representation, training-only standardization and donor-paired endpoint sampling supported separate timepoint-holdout and donor-transfer protocols. We compared four time coordinates, shared and donor-conditioned flow matching, three non-neural predictors, two training-donor counts and three training seeds. Three mouse model arms supplied a separate computational audit.
Pooled Wound7 reconstruction reduced energy distance relative to stay-still by +49.9\%. Excluding each donor preserved the result for the retained donors in 3/3 sensitivity runs; genuine held-out-donor transfer separately improved by a mean +75.9\% in 3/3 donors. Linear days failed at the target (−18.2\%), whereas rank, square-root and log-day coordinates improved. All five predictive methods met the specified split-half-reference criterion in 3/3 donor folds. Within the donor-transfer benchmark, shared flow had the smallest mean error, 0.395; conditioning improved all six training-donor comparisons without improving the overall held-out mean. Adding a second training donor reduced seed-0 mean error from 0.5496 to 0.3854. Aggregate reductions were 0.156--0.176 across seeds, with donor-block interval [0.081, 0.315], but some individual configurations varied by 0.368. In additional global day-7 and day-1 holdouts, centroid translation had lower mean donor error than shared flow (0.667 versus 0.811 and 0.929 versus 1.057, respectively). Mouse-arm changes ranged from −1.5\% to +50.6\%, with one animal per arm and time point.
These experiments support bounded reconstruction in a three-donor acute-wound pilot. The simple interpolation controls show that a neural field is not required for the two globally missing-time tasks tested here. They establish neither DFU prognosis nor a population-level method ranking, identifiable biological trajectories, or a required cohort size.
\par\smallskip\noindent Keywords: conditional flow matching; wound healing; held-out donor; energy distance; time coordinate; donor heterogeneity.

\section{Introduction}
Wound repair involves changing populations of inflammatory, stromal and epithelial cells, together with changes in the activity of those cells \citep{wilkinson2020}. Experimental studies show that fibroblast proliferation, matrix deposition and interactions with macrophages contribute to the organization of repairing skin \citep{rognoni2018,shook2018}. These processes motivate a temporal description of wound tissue, but they do not imply that all wounds follow a common transcriptional clock. Acute repair in healthy skin and the course of a diabetic foot ulcer differ in their clinical setting, and a model of one cannot be assumed to predict the other \citep{armstrong2017}.

Single-cell sequencing provides detailed observations of these populations while destroying the cells being measured. Samples collected at successive times therefore describe distributions of cell states rather than repeated observations of individual cells. More than one dynamic process can connect the same marginal distributions, which limits what can be identified from snapshots alone \citep{weinreb2018}. Combining transcriptional profiles with experimental lineage tracing can connect state to subsequent fate and supply information absent from expression snapshots \citep{weinreb2020}. Without such information, a smooth computational trajectory should be interpreted in terms of its stated assumptions and prediction target.

Different single-cell methods use different sources of temporal information. Trajectory inference organizes cells according to a modeled state-space topology or progression \citep{saelens2019}. RNA velocity uses spliced and unspliced transcript information to estimate local expression change, with dynamical extensions relaxing particular steady-state assumptions \citep{lamanno2018,bergen2020}. CellRank combines local directional information with a probabilistic description of fate transitions \citep{lange2022}. These approaches address questions related to, but distinct from, predicting the distribution of cells recovered from a later tissue specimen. Their outputs cannot be treated as equivalent observations of longitudinal biological motion.

Distributional models instead specify how one population measure is related to another. Optimal transport uses a chosen cost and coupling to connect observations \citep{schiebinger2019,peyre2019}, while neural differential equations represent a continuous evolution in state space \citep{chen2018ode,tong2020trajectory}. Flow matching learns a vector field from a prescribed family of paths and conditional regression targets \citep{lipman2023,tong2023,lipman2024guide}. Neural stochastic differential equations additionally model diffusion, with recent work evaluating such models against lineage-informed trajectories \citep{vinyard2025}. Related transport models have also been used for perturbation prediction \citep{bunne2023}. These developments expand the modeling choices; they do not remove the need to test whether a selected model predicts observations withheld from its construction.

That test depends on what is withheld. Removing one time point from every donor asks whether the model can reconstruct an unobserved part of the temporal sequence. Removing one donor while retaining all times in the others asks whether relationships learned from those donors transfer to the excluded person. In the latter setting, the observed source distribution of the test donor can initialize prediction without supplying its target. Neither design is equivalent to randomly separating cells from the same specimens. Cross-validation for structured data needs to reflect the dependence and extrapolation relevant to the intended application \citep{roberts2017}. The prediction task therefore has to be defined before model performance can be interpreted.

The biological object of prediction should also be made explicit. An observed fibroblast distribution is recovered from one sampled tissue region at one time; its transcriptional variation can reflect activation, composition within the compartment and technical recovery. Descriptive expression profiles help connect latent coordinates to that material without converting the coordinates into a spatial wound map. A mean translation between available samples provides a particularly direct baseline: it asks whether moving the center of the observed population is sufficient for the evaluated target, before attributing predictive value to a flexible nonlinear field. Such a baseline must receive the same permitted donor and temporal information as the model it is compared with.

Evaluation also depends on the representation and probability measure being compared. A latent expression map makes distributional calculations tractable but determines which differences contribute to distance \citep{lopez2018}. Quality control and normalization also determine which cells and variation enter that comparison \citep{heumos2023}. Experience with atlas integration shows that preserving biological variation and removing technical differences can be competing objectives \citep{luecken2022}. Energy distance supplies a distributional discrepancy with established connections to kernel-based testing \citep{szekely2013,sejdinovic2013}, but a distance in latent coordinates is not automatically a distance in clinical severity or gene-specific function. Pooling cells weights donors according to their recovered cell counts unless weights are changed explicitly. Preprocessing, weighting and a simple unchanged-source baseline are consequently part of the prediction question rather than incidental implementation choices.

The number of independent donors constrains what repeated computational evaluation can establish. Many cells improve the empirical description of each sampled population, while leaving donor-level biological replication unchanged \citep{zimmerman2021}. Training seeds, integration resolutions and cell-sampling draws examine other sources of variability. Model selection on the same small held-out set can still lead to optimistic estimates, even when the fitting procedure is reproducible \citep{varma2006,cawley2010}. Small-sample validation studies also show why performance estimates require caution when the data support many analytical choices but few independent test units \citep{vabalas2019}. These considerations favor a clearly bounded reconstruction task with explicit controls over a claim of unrestricted clinical generalization.

The acute human wound atlas supplies four measurement times from three healthy volunteers \citep{liu2025_wound,geo_gse241132}. We use this setting to ask whether moving an observed day-1 distribution under a fitted field brings it closer to held-out day-7 cells than leaving the source unchanged. Separate timepoint and donor exclusions define the two prediction tasks. Comparisons of time coordinates, shared and donor-conditioned fields, non-neural predictors, training-donor counts and seeds examine the dependence on modeling choices. Fixed-field integration, evaluation sampling and donor weighting address additional computational and measurement choices. Mouse time courses provide a separate comparison under different biological and replication conditions. The aim is to establish what this limited design can test about distributional prediction before extending its interpretation to individual wound progression.

\section*{Data and reproducibility}
Human acute-wound data and author cell annotations are available as GSE241132 \citep{liu2025_wound,geo_gse241132}. GSE165816 supplies the independently frozen expression model \citep{theocharidis2022}; its DFU clinical labels are not used to train the human temporal fields. Mouse data are from GSE326622 \citep{geo_gse326622}. This secondary computational study recruited no participants and performed no new animal experiments. Source-study consent and ethical approvals remain those of the original investigators. Sample identities, preprocessing exclusions, numerical settings and all reported comparisons are defined in the methods and supplementary tables.
