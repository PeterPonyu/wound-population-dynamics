\section{Discussion}
The experiments support reconstruction of one observed acute-wound interval in a small human cohort. A shared population field improved on an unchanged source distribution when the target time was globally withheld and when a donor was withheld under a separate transfer protocol. These tasks answer different questions: the former lacks target-time observations in every donor, while the latter can learn that time from other donors. Their agreement is informative about this representation and cohort, but does not establish population-wide clinical generalization.

The time-coordinate comparison shows that an observation schedule is part of the model specification. A linear-day axis performed poorly at the specified target even when alternative coordinates improved. This sensitivity is consistent with compressing early changes into a short interval of the global time coordinate. It does not identify a universal biological clock. Distributional transport and flow matching offer flexible parameterizations \citep{schiebinger2019,tong2020trajectory,lipman2024guide}, while biological ancestry remains underdetermined without additional information \citep{weinreb2018,lavenant2021}.

The model comparison separates fitting capacity from transfer. Donor conditioning improved the fit to donors represented in training, but the shared field had the lowest mean error on held-out donors. Simple non-neural displacement methods also improved prediction, including when a second training donor was added. The evidence therefore supports useful shared structure without attributing the whole improvement to neural complexity. A future comparison should assess performance in additional donors under a fixed protocol, including simple predictors and methods that model context explicitly \citep{bunne2023}.

The donor-count experiment identifies a gain at the two measured training sizes. Its aggregate direction persisted across seeds, while individual donor and training-subset configurations varied substantially. One training donor already met the stated empirical criterion. Two observed sizes cannot establish a learning-curve plateau or a required cohort size, and overlapping training sets limit the independence of the fold summaries. Extending the curve requires additional independent donors and a plateau criterion defined before inspecting the expanded data.

The added computational sensitivities distinguish integration error from evaluation variability. Increasing integration resolution changed the target error negligibly for the tested fixed field. Repeated evaluation subsamples at three cell counts also preserved the improvement over the unchanged-source baseline. These findings reduce concern about those particular numerical choices. They do not include uncertainty in the frozen representation, variation between future donors, or a complete repetition of model training. Energy-distance comparison remains conditional on coordinates, cell sampling and the selected target \citep{szekely2013}.

The human cohort contains only three healthy donors and four times. Cells and repeated subsamples do not increase the number of biological replications \citep{zimmerman2021}. The mouse arms each contain one animal per time point, differ in predictive performance, and have incomplete gene-panel coverage. Their comparison assesses computational behavior across distinct experimental contexts; it does not establish cross-species equivalence. The available data also contain no diabetic-foot-ulcer trajectory with an authoritative time-to-healing endpoint. The next biological test should therefore expand independent-donor evaluation before using an explicitly longitudinal diabetic-foot-ulcer cohort to assess patient-level staging or prognosis.
